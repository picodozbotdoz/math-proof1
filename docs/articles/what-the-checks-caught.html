What the checks caught: a catalogue of confident mistakes

False anchors, an unsound certificate scheme, a false group-theory lemma and a theorem Mathlib already had. Each was stated with confidence, and each was stopped by a check.

This project is run largely by AI agents, and much of the work starts from written plans. The plans were often wrong, in confident and plausible ways. Nothing false reached the proof library, because of the layers of checks around it. The list below is the record of what those checks stopped, mostly from the project's own logs, plus one thing they did not stop. We think it is more useful than a list of successes.

The hardest case: when the plan itself is the bug

The most instructive failure was not a false statement. During Iteration 21 the internal agents stalled twice on the Galois work. An external AI session then finished it in one sitting. The project's own retrospective is frank about why, and the reasons were not about ability.

  • It compiled the inherited work first. The notes described a 300 to 400-line task. The compiler showed about 20 errors, most of them trivial, such as a missing import. The internal analysis had treated those as deep problems.
  • It re-derived the plan. The plan carried a resultant-transport step inherited from the original blueprint. Separability of the reduction already gives distinct roots, a one-line fact, so the whole step could go.
  • It started with fresh context, where the stalled agent had accumulated about 125 million tokens. Still, it built on the project's checkpoints, certificates and notes, so the result was cumulative.

The good habit of writing everything down had turned into trusting what was written. The notes outlived a host reboot and two stalls, and that durability is what gave them unearned authority.

The rules that came out of this

  1. Compile the artifact before believing the document.
  2. Treat blueprints as hypotheses to test, not plans to execute.
  3. Treat probe and census data as untrusted input to a check, never as ground truth. Re-derive it by a second route.
  4. Search by concept, not by name, before building anything large that a library "probably lacks". That goes for neighbouring libraries too, and a survey is only as good as its date.
  5. Claim "clean" only from a green build.
  6. Start fresh agents on documented plans rather than continuing exhausted ones.
THE LIMIT OF ALL THIS

Checks catch what they check. The TauCeti entry above shows the gap: no check fires when a statement about what does not exist is wrong, and it only cost duplicated work. Lean guarantees that the proof proves the stated theorem. It does not guarantee that the prose around it is right, and that includes this site. Every claim of "first formalization" here is a judgment, not a theorem. Numbers in these articles were rechecked against the source and in some cases recomputed, but only the Lean statements have the kernel's guarantee.
